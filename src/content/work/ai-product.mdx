## The Problem

Clinical research documentation is enormous. A single study protocol can run hundreds of pages. Add amendments, site-specific procedures, regulatory guidance, and visit records, and you have a documentation landscape that's nearly impossible to navigate quickly.

The people who need to navigate it fastest — coordinators managing visits, monitors reviewing data, investigators making decisions — are also the most time-constrained. The standard answer was "read the protocol." Nobody had time to read the protocol.

The result was a predictable pattern: questions went unanswered, decisions got made on incomplete information, and errors that could have been caught upstream surfaced downstream where they were expensive to fix.

## The Approach

The insight that shaped the product: the information existed. It was in the system. The problem was retrieval — getting the right piece of information to the right person at the right moment, in a form they could act on.

Large language models are good at this. But clinical research has a trust problem with AI — and for good reason. A coordinator who gets a wrong answer from an AI assistant and acts on it can create a protocol deviation. The answer wasn't just accurate AI. It was verifiable AI.

Every response the assistant generates includes a citation: the exact document, section, or record the answer came from. Coordinators can check the source in one click. The assistant doesn't just answer — it shows its work.

## Key Decisions

**Citation-first responses.** Every factual claim the assistant makes is linked to a source. This wasn't an afterthought — it was the core design constraint. An assistant without citations is an assistant you can't trust.

**Generative UI.** Some questions are better answered with structured data than prose. "What visits are coming up?" shouldn't return a paragraph — it should return a list. The assistant detects when a structured response is more useful and generates the appropriate UI widget inline.

**Scoped to study context.** The assistant knows which study you're working in. It doesn't answer in generalities — it answers about your participants, your protocol, your data. Context-aware responses are more useful and less likely to be misapplied.
